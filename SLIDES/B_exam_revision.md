# Exam Revision

*Audience: students preparing for end-of-semester or end-of-year exams.*
*Format: 6 slides. Total talk time: ~8 minutes.*

---

## Slide 1 — Revision is not re-reading

**The single biggest revision mistake**

Most students "revise" by re-reading notes and textbook chapters. This feels productive. It does almost nothing. Memory is built by **retrieval** — pulling the answer out of your head, not putting it in again.

**What actually works**
- **Active recall** — close the book, write down everything you remember, then check
- **Spaced repetition** — revisit the material in growing intervals, not all at once
- **Past papers under timed conditions** — the single highest-value revision activity

*Speaker note: Open with the punchline. Half the room is re-reading their notes right now. Say that out loud and watch them close the notebook.*

---

## Slide 2 — What exams actually test

**Three skills, in order of how often they're examined**

1. **Recall** — can you define this term, state this rule, list these steps?
2. **Application** — can you use the concept in a problem you haven't seen before?
3. **Analysis** — can you compare, critique, or extend?

**Examples across disciplines**
- **CS112 Data Structures** — recall: what a stack does. Application: write a function that reverses a string using one. Analysis: why is a stack the wrong choice for a queue problem?
- **BU202 Cost Accounting** — recall: fixed vs. variable cost. Application: compute break-even for a small business. Analysis: what happens to break-even if you outsource half the production?
- **AR111 Research Methods** — recall: what a sample is. Application: design a small survey. Analysis: where would your design fail?

*Speaker note: Exams almost never test only recall at higher semesters. Show the audience the ladder — recall, application, analysis — and tell them which rung their exam sits on.*

## Slide 3 — A worked revision sequence

**How to revise a topic in 45 minutes**

- **0–5 min** — Write down everything you already know on the topic, from memory. No notes.
- **5–15 min** — Open your notes. Add what you missed. Highlight the gaps.
- **15–30 min** — Do 2–3 practice problems on that topic, closed-book.
- **30–40 min** — Mark your own work. For every mistake, write the correct reasoning next to it.
- **40–45 min** — Write a one-paragraph summary of the topic. This becomes your revision sheet.

**Repeat for each topic.** Two topics per evening is enough. Five is too many and you'll retain less.

*Speaker note: This is the practical heart of the deck. Students don't revise badly because they're lazy. They revise badly because nobody told them the method. Give the method.*

---

## Slide 4 — Three mistakes that cost marks

**1. Studying what you already know**
It feels good. It's the most common revision trap. Spend 80% of your time on the 20% you find hard.

**2. Practising without timing**
An exam has a clock. If you've never done a problem under time pressure, the exam room will surprise you. Practise at speed, at least twice before the real thing.

**3. Reading the model answer and thinking "yes, I would have written that"**
You wouldn't. Not under pressure, from memory. Write your answer first, **then** look at the model. If you skip the writing, you've learned nothing.

*Speaker note: The third one is the most important. Most students "study" by reading model answers. It's the least effective method on the list. Say it plainly.*

## Slide 5 — The week before

**A seven-day revision plan**

| Day | Focus |
|---|---|
| 7 days out | Map every topic onto a single page. Rate your confidence 1–5. |
| 6 | Revise the two lowest-rated topics. Do 5 practice problems. |
| 5 | Revise the next two lowest. Another 5 problems. |
| 4 | Full past paper, timed. Mark it yourself. |
| 3 | Fix every mistake from yesterday. |
| 2 | Second past paper, timed. Different year. |
| 1 | Light review only. Sleep early. |
| Exam day | Eat, arrive early, breathe. |

*Speaker note: Sleep is not a luxury in this plan. Students who cram the night before lose more marks to fatigue than they gain from the extra study.*

---

## Slide 6 — What to remember

**Three sentences**

1. **Revision is retrieval, not re-reading. Close the book and check what you actually remember.**
2. **Practise under time pressure — the exam has a clock, and your practice should too.**
3. **The night before, sleep beats more study.**

**What comes next**
- After the exam, review what you got wrong. The exam is data — it tells you where next semester to focus.
- If you scored lower than you expected on a topic, that topic is the one to review before the next course builds on it.
- Bring your marked paper to your faculty. Ask them to walk through one answer with you. That conversation is worth more than another hour of solo study.

*Speaker note: End by turning the exam from a verdict into information. Students who treat exams as feedback improve; students who treat them as judgement don't.*
