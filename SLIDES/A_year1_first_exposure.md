# Year 1 — First Exposure

*Audience: first-year students encountering a discipline for the first time.*
*Format: 6 slides. Total talk time: ~8 minutes.*

---

## Slide 1 — Welcome to your first year

**Why the first year matters more than any other**

- Everything you learn in Year 1 becomes the vocabulary for Years 2–4
- The habits you build now — how you take notes, how you ask for help, how you study — stay with you
- Year 1 is not a filter. It is a foundation. Nobody expects you to know anything yet.

**Examples across disciplines**
- **CS101 Programming Fundamentals** — you learn what a variable is, and why a computer can't think for itself
- **BU103 Financial Accounting** — you learn what a balance sheet is, and why a business is not its bank account
- **AR101 Academic Writing** — you learn what a thesis is, and why an essay is an argument, not a summary

*Speaker note: The first slide is reassurance. Most Year 1 students are afraid of being "behind." They are not behind — they are at the beginning. Say that out loud.*

---

## Slide 2 — The five things every subject asks you to do

**Across every discipline, Year 1 work comes down to five tasks**

1. **Learn the vocabulary** — every field has words that mean something specific. "Demand" in economics is not "demand" in ordinary speech. Learn the precise meaning.
2. **Recognise the shape of a problem** — is this a "find the pattern" problem, a "compare two things" problem, or a "prove something is true" problem?
3. **Follow a method** — every subject has a canonical way to work. In accounting it's double-entry. In programming it's write-test-fix. In writing it's outline-draft-revise.
4. **Ask for help early** — the students who struggle are not the ones who don't understand. They are the ones who don't ask.
5. **Practise deliberately** — reading about a loop is not the same as writing one.

*Speaker note: This is the slide that changes behaviour. Take 60 seconds here. Emphasise "ask for help early" — that is the single strongest predictor of who does well in Year 1.*

---

## Slide 3 — A worked example: the same skill in three subjects

**"Break the problem into parts"**

- **CS101** — Write a program that prints the first 10 even numbers.
  - Break into: (a) how to count, (b) how to test evenness, (c) how to stop at 10.
- **BU103** — Prepare a simple balance sheet for a new business.
  - Break into: (a) list assets, (b) list liabilities, (c) compute owner's equity.
- **AR101** — Write a 500-word argument that social media harms attention.
  - Break into: (a) state the claim, (b) list two supporting reasons, (c) address one counter-argument.

**The pattern:** every Year 1 problem gets easier the moment you stop trying to solve it whole and start listing the sub-problems.

*Speaker note: Say the "pattern" line slowly. The goal of this slide is not to teach any one subject — it is to teach that a strategy transfers across all of them.*

---

## Slide 4 — Three mistakes that sink Year 1 students

**1. Studying by reading**
Reading your notes feels productive. It isn't. You learn by doing — solving, writing, practising. If you can't do the problem without looking, you don't know it yet.

**2. Skipping the basics because they seem too simple**
The students who fail Data Structures usually didn't fail Data Structures. They failed the loops and conditionals from CS101 that Data Structures assumes. The basics are not a warm-up. They are the thing.

**3. Treating the first bad mark as a verdict**
One failed quiz in October is not a prediction of your degree. It is data. Look at it, adjust, move on.

*Speaker note: These three land hardest if you give a concrete example. "I once saw a student fail CS201 because they never learned what an array index was" is more memorable than the abstraction.*

---

## Slide 5 — Practice: what to do this week

**Five things you can do in the next 7 days**

1. Rewrite your notes from one lecture as **questions and answers** instead of bullet points. Quiz yourself tomorrow.
2. Pick one problem from each subject and solve it **without looking at the solution**. Then compare.
3. Ask one question in class, or email one professor. Even if it feels obvious.
4. Find one other student in each subject and form a two-person study group.
5. Write down, in one sentence, what each subject is *actually about*.

*Speaker note: These are concrete and small on purpose. If a student does even two of the five, they will feel the difference by the end of the term.*

---

## Slide 6 — What to remember

**Three sentences to carry through the year**

1. **Your job in Year 1 is not to know things — it is to learn how to learn them.**
2. **Ask for help on the first day you notice you need it, not the last.**
3. **Reading is not studying. Doing is studying.**

**What comes next**
- Year 2 will assume you can read a formula, write a paragraph of clean reasoning, and use a computer for basic work.
- If you can do those three things by the end of Year 1, Year 2 will feel manageable.
- If you cannot yet, ask your faculty for help now — before the gap widens.

*Speaker note: End on the "what comes next" section. It gives the audience a reason to take the earlier slides seriously. Nothing motivates Year 1 like knowing what Year 2 expects.*
