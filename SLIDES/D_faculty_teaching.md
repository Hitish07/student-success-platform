# Faculty Teaching Guide

*Audience: faculty planning and delivering a course.*
*Format: 6 slides. Total talk time: ~8 minutes.*

---

## Slide 1 — What makes a course work

**A good course has four things**

1. **Clear outcomes** — at the end, students should be able to *do* specific things, not just *know* specific facts
2. **Consistent practice** — students should solve problems every week, not just listen
3. **Visible feedback** — students should know how they're doing before the exam, not after
4. **A clear path from theory to application** — every abstract concept should touch at least one concrete example

**The test of a well-designed course:** could a student who attended every class and did every exercise predict their own grade within 5% before the exam? If not, feedback is too infrequent.

*Speaker note: The last line is the sharpest tool in this slide. If your students are surprised by their grades, that's on the course design, not on the students.*

## Slide 2 — Writing clear learning outcomes

**Bad outcome:** "Students will understand data structures."

**Good outcomes:**
- Students will **implement** a stack and a queue from scratch
- Students will **choose** the appropriate data structure for a given problem and justify the choice
- Students will **analyse** the time complexity of their implementation

**The rule:** every outcome starts with a verb that names a behaviour. **Understand** and **know** are not behaviours — they're invisible. **Implement**, **compare**, **design**, **evaluate**, **justify** are.

**Examples across disciplines**
- **BU212 Operations Management** — "Students will compute EOQ for a given demand and holding cost and explain when the model breaks down."
- **AR111 Research Methods** — "Students will design a small survey, pilot it on 10 respondents, and revise it based on the pilot."
- **EN101 Foundations of Engineering** — "Students will build and test a simple truss structure and calculate the load it can bear."

*Speaker note: If a slide has only one actionable takeaway, this is it. Rewrite every outcome to start with a verb. It changes how you teach the course.*

## Slide 3 — A lesson plan template

**90-minute session**

| Minutes | Activity |
|---|---|
| 0–5 | Reconnect — one question from last session, one new question for today |
| 5–20 | Concept introduction — 15 minutes, no more. Keep it tight. |
| 20–35 | Worked example — do it on the board, narrate your reasoning |
| 35–55 | Students solve a related problem, in pairs. Walk the room. |
| 55–70 | Debrief — 2–3 pairs share their approach. Discuss differences. |
| 70–85 | Second concept, or a harder version of the same problem |
| 85–90 | Summarise. Assign one thing to do before next session. |

**The one rule:** students should be doing something for at least half the session. If they're listening for more than 45 minutes, they've stopped learning and started writing.

*Speaker note: The 20-minute "students working" block is the part most lessons skip when time runs short. It's also the part that produces the learning. Protect it.*

## Slide 4 — Common teaching mistakes

**1. Explaining the same thing louder**
If a student didn't understand, repeating the same explanation at higher volume doesn't help. Change the example. Change the analogy. Ask the student to explain what they *do* understand.

**2. Solving the problem for them**
The urge to grab the pen and finish the derivation is strong. Resist it. Ask: "what's the next step?" and let them find it.

**3. Never checking for understanding until the exam**
Ask two questions every session that require every student to answer. Show of hands, mini-quiz, one-sentence summary. If you don't know what they know, you're teaching blind.

**4. Treating silence as comprehension**
When you ask "any questions?" and nobody raises a hand, that is not agreement. It is often confusion. Ask instead: "what's the one part of this that's least clear?"

*Speaker note: The fourth one is the most common. "Any questions?" always gets silence. Change the question and you'll get answers.*

## Slide 5 — Assessment and feedback

**Three principles**

1. **Frequent, small, low-stakes** — weekly 10-minute quizzes teach more than two big exams
2. **Feedback before the exam, not after** — if students only learn how they're doing when the grade comes back, it's too late to change anything
3. **Feedback on the *process*, not just the answer** — "you used the wrong formula" is less useful than "you chose the right formula but mis-simplified in step 3"

**A simple structure**
- Weekly quiz (10 minutes, 5 marks)
- Midterm (30% of grade)
- Final (40%)
- Project or problem set (30%)

Adjust the weights to your subject, but keep at least one low-stakes check every week.

*Speaker note: If you can only change one thing, add the weekly quiz. It moves grades more than any other change a teacher can make.*

---

## Slide 6 — What to remember

**Three sentences**

1. **Every outcome starts with a verb that names a behaviour. If you can't see it, you can't assess it.**
2. **Students should work for at least half the session — that's where learning happens.**
3. **Feedback must arrive before the exam to matter. Weekly, small, low-stakes.**

**What comes next**
- After every semester, look at the *distribution* of grades. If everything is clustered at C, the course isn't discriminating. If everything is A, it isn't assessing.
- Ask students what they found hardest. The answer is often not what you expected.
- Bring one change into the next delivery. One change per semester compounds.

*Speaker note: End on the "one change per semester" line. Teaching improves in small steps, not revolutions, and faculty need permission to iterate slowly.*
